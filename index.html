Gasoline-Price Inflation and Grocery Price Changes

A comparison of perishable and nonperishable food categories in the United States from 2019 to 2025

By Selena Long

View Results Read Full Report

About the Study

Research Question

How does gasoline-price inflation affect price changes in perishable compared to nonperishable grocery categories in the United States from 2019 to 2025?

Background

Gasoline and groceries are two major costs that many families notice. Gasoline prices can change quickly because of oil prices, refinery problems, supply shortages, and world events.

Grocery prices usually change more slowly, but food still has to be transported, refrigerated, stored, and stocked. This study examined whether gasoline-price inflation was connected to grocery price changes.

Why the Study Matters

Higher fuel costs may increase the cost of transporting food. Perishable foods may be affected more because they often need faster delivery and refrigeration.

This study tested whether perishable grocery categories were more affected by gasoline changes than nonperishable grocery categories.

Dataset

Data Used in the Study

The data came from the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics Consumer Price Index database. The study used monthly CPI-U data for the U.S. city average.

Methods

How the Data Was Analyzed

01

Data Preparation

The dates were placed in monthly order and the CPI values were converted into numbers.

02

Trend Analysis

The study compared how gasoline and food CPI values changed between 2019 and 2025.

03

Correlation

Monthly percentage changes were compared to see whether gasoline and grocery prices moved together.

04

SARIMA Models

A separate SARIMA forecasting model was created for each CPI category.

05

VAR Model

One VAR model analyzed all seven categories together and used past values from every category.

06

Relationship Tests

Granger-causality tests and impulse-response functions were used to study whether gasoline changes helped predict later food-price changes.

Training and Testing Periods

Training Period

January 2019 through December 2024

72 months

Testing Period

January 2025 through December 2025

12 months

The final 12 months were not used to build the models. They were saved to test how accurately the models predicted data they had not already seen.

Results

Main Findings

Gasoline had the largest price swings.

Gasoline changed much more from month to month than the grocery categories. It dropped sharply in early 2020 and then increased quickly during 2021 and 2022.

All categories increased over the study period.

Sweets and candy increased the most, followed by meat and eggs and nonalcoholic beverages. Food categories generally followed steadier upward trends than gasoline.

Gasoline and grocery price changes had weak relationships.

Most monthly correlations were close to zero. This means gasoline and grocery prices did not usually move together from one month to the next.

Meat and eggs was the only significant category.

Gasoline-price changes significantly helped predict later changes in meat and egg prices. The p-value was 0.006.

Gasoline did not significantly predict dairy, fruits and vegetables, cereals and bakery products, nonalcoholic beverages, or sweets and candy.

The impulse-response results showed that grocery prices changed very little after a sudden gasoline price shock.

Limitations

Limits of the Study

Small Dataset

The study only included 84 monthly observations, which may make small effects harder to detect.

Limited Variables

The study did not include other factors such as wages, oil prices, farming costs, interest rates, or supply-chain indexes.

Estimated Data

October 2025 values for the six food categories were estimated instead of directly observed.

Unusual Events

The pandemic and avian influenza may have affected gasoline and food prices during the study period.

No Proof of Cause

Correlation and Granger causality show relationships, but they do not prove that gasoline directly caused food-price changes.

Conclusion

What the Study Found

Gasoline prices were not the main reason grocery prices changed from 2019 to 2025.

Gasoline-price inflation had only a small effect on most grocery categories. Meat and eggs was the only category with a clear predictive relationship to gasoline prices.

Perishable foods may have been affected slightly more, but the overall difference between perishable and nonperishable foods was still small.

Neither forecasting model worked best for every category. VAR performed better for four categories, while SARIMA performed better for three categories.

Other factors such as farming costs, labor, packaging, supply problems, and overall inflation also affected grocery prices.
